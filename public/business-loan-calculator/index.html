How a business term loan payment works

A term loan is a fixed amount borrowed once and repaid over a set number of months. Most are amortizing, which means each payment is the same and the balance reaches zero with the last one. Each payment covers that month's interest first, and whatever is left reduces the balance, called the principal. Early payments are mostly interest. By the final year almost the whole payment goes to principal. The schedule above shows that shift year by year.

Three inputs drive the payment: the amount borrowed, the annual interest rate, and the number of months. The calculator converts the annual rate to a monthly rate by dividing by 12, then applies the standard amortization formula shown under "How it's calculated."

Worked example

Say a business borrows $150,000 at 9.5% for five years to buy equipment and fund a second location.

  • Monthly rate: 9.5% ÷ 12 = about 0.79% a month.
  • Number of payments: 5 years × 12 = 60.
  • Monthly payment: $3,150.28.
  • Total of all payments: $3,150.28 × 60 = $189,016.75.
  • Total interest: $189,016.75 − $150,000 = $39,016.75.

In the first month, interest is $150,000 × 0.79%, or about $1,187.50, so only about $1,963 of the first payment reduces the balance. Over the first year the business pays $13,196.90 in interest and $24,606.45 in principal, leaving a balance of $125,393.55. In year five, interest drops to $1,875.51 and principal paid rises to $35,927.84.

Term versus total cost

Stretching the term lowers the payment but raises total interest, often by more than people expect. Run the same $150,000 at 9.5% over seven years instead of five:

  • The payment falls to $2,451.60, about $699 a month less.
  • Total interest rises to $55,934.17, about $16,900 more.

Neither answer is wrong. A lower payment leaves more cash for operations and makes the loan easier to qualify for. A shorter term costs less overall. A reasonable approach is to choose the shortest term your cash flow can carry comfortably in a slow month, not just an average one.

What the quote leaves out

The interest rate is not the whole cost. Lenders commonly add an origination fee, a packaging or documentation fee, and sometimes a prepayment penalty. If a fee is deducted from the loan proceeds, you pay interest on money you never receive.

In the example above, suppose the lender charges a 2% origination fee, $3,000, taken out of the proceeds. The business receives $147,000 but still repays $3,150.28 a month on $150,000. Measured against the cash actually received, the annual cost works out to about 10.4%, not 9.5%. That is why you should ask each lender for the APR, which folds required fees into one annual rate, when you compare offers.

  • Origination fee: usually a percentage of the loan, paid at closing or taken from proceeds.
  • Prepayment penalty: a charge for paying the loan off early. Ask whether it steps down over time.
  • Variable rate: if the rate floats with an index such as prime, your payment can change. Run this calculator at a rate one or two points higher to see the risk. At 11.5%, the example payment rises to about $3,299, roughly $149 more each month.

Can the business afford the payment?

Lenders answer that question with the debt service coverage ratio (DSCR): cash flow available for debt divided by total annual debt payments. Many want at least 1.20x to 1.25x. In the example, the new loan adds $3,150.28 × 12 = $37,803 a year of debt service. Add your other loan payments and run the total through the DSCR calculator before you apply. If the ratio comes up short, a longer term or a smaller amount is easier to fix now than after a decline.

Using the schedule

The interest column matters for taxes and budgeting. Interest on a loan used for business purposes is generally deductible as a business expense, so your tax preparer will want the annual interest figure. Principal payments are not an expense, which is why a business can show a profit and still feel short of cash while repaying a loan.

The ending balance column tells you roughly what it would take to pay the loan off or refinance at the end of any year, before any prepayment penalty. If you expect to sell the business, replace equipment or refinance within a few years, that number is often more useful than the payment.

Before you talk to a lender

  1. Know exactly how much you need and what it is for. Lenders want a use of funds, not a round number.
  2. Match the term to the life of what you are buying. A five-year asset should not sit on a ten-year loan, or a ten-year asset on a two-year loan.
  3. Have two to three years of tax returns, current financial statements and a list of existing debts ready.
  4. Get every quote in writing with the rate, term, fees and any prepayment terms, then compare them on the same basis.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate a business loan payment?

Use the standard amortization formula: payment equals the loan amount times the monthly rate, divided by one minus one plus the monthly rate raised to the negative number of months. The calculator does this for you and shows the result as you type.

Is a longer term better?

A longer term lowers the monthly payment and makes the loan easier to qualify for, but you pay more interest in total. Choose the shortest term your cash flow can comfortably carry.

Does this include fees?

No. It calculates principal and interest only. Add origination and other fees separately, or ask the lender for the APR to compare offers.

Is business loan interest tax deductible?

Interest on a loan used for business purposes is generally deductible. Check with your tax preparer for your situation.
