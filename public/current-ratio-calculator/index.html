How it's calculated

What these ratios show

Liquidity ratios answer a simple question: if every bill due in the next twelve months came due, could the business pay it from assets that turn into cash within the same year?

  • Current ratio compares all current assets to current liabilities. 1.50 means $1.50 of short-term assets for every $1.00 of short-term obligations.
  • Quick ratio counts only cash and receivables, leaving out inventory and prepaid expenses, which can take time to convert or can't be converted at all.
  • Working capital is the dollar difference, the cushion the business operates on.

What lenders look for

Many commercial lenders like a current ratio of at least 1.20 to 1.50 and a quick ratio near 1.00, and some write minimums into loan covenants. Context matters: a distributor carrying heavy inventory will have a much higher current ratio than quick ratio, while a service business may have almost no inventory, so the two ratios are close.

Common reasons the ratios look weak

  1. Long-term needs funded with short-term money. Buying equipment or a vehicle out of the line of credit puts long-lived assets against current debt. Term out the borrowing instead.
  2. Current portion of long-term debt. A loan maturing within twelve months moves entirely into current liabilities. Refinance before year end if possible.
  3. Owner distributions. Large draws reduce cash directly.
  4. Slow collections or excess inventory, which inflate the current ratio while the quick ratio stays weak. Check your cash conversion cycle.

A ratio is a snapshot

Balance sheet ratios reflect a single day, so they can swing with timing, such as a large customer payment arriving the day after month end. Track them monthly from consistent, reconciled books, and look at the trend rather than one reading.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good current ratio?

Between about 1.20 and 2.00 is generally healthy for small businesses. Below 1.00 means current liabilities exceed current assets.

What is the difference between the current ratio and the quick ratio?

The quick ratio leaves out inventory and prepaid expenses, counting only cash and receivables, so it is a stricter test of liquidity.

Can a current ratio be too high?

Yes. A very high ratio can mean cash is sitting idle or inventory and receivables are building up instead of turning into cash.

Is the line of credit balance a current liability?

Usually, yes. Revolving lines are typically due within a year and are classified as current liabilities.
