What the cash conversion cycle tells you

The cash conversion cycle (CCC) measures how many days pass between paying for inventory and collecting cash from the customer who bought it. A 55-day cycle means every dollar you put into inventory is unavailable for about 55 days. The longer the cycle, the more working capital the business needs, and the more it has to borrow as it grows.

The cycle has three parts:

  • Days inventory outstanding (DIO): how long inventory sits before it sells.
  • Days sales outstanding (DSO): how long customers take to pay.
  • Days payables outstanding (DPO): how long you take to pay suppliers. This one shortens the cycle, because your suppliers are financing you in the meantime.

Why growing businesses run out of cash

A profitable business can still run short of cash if its cycle is long. Each new sale requires buying inventory now and collecting later. The "cash tied up" figure above estimates how much money is locked in the cycle at your current cost base. If sales grow 25%, that figure grows about 25% too, and the cash has to come from somewhere.

Ways to shorten the cycle

  1. Collect faster: invoice on the day you ship, offer card or ACH payment, follow up on day one past due, and consider a small early-payment discount.
  2. Carry less inventory: find slow-moving items and stop reordering them; order more often in smaller quantities.
  3. Pay on terms, not early: negotiate 45 or 60 days with key suppliers and use the full term, unless an early-pay discount is worth more.

Even a few days off the cycle can free a meaningful amount of cash. At $1.1 million in annual cost of goods, each day is worth about $3,000.

Using the right numbers

Use a full year of revenue and cost of goods sold, and average balances for inventory, receivables and payables if your business is seasonal. A single month-end balance can make the cycle look better or worse than it is. Lenders who finance working capital, especially asset-based lenders, look at these same measures.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good cash conversion cycle?

It depends on the industry. Retailers and distributors often run 30 to 90 days; businesses paid up front can run negative. Compare yourself to your own trend and to peers.

Can the cash conversion cycle be negative?

Yes. If customers pay before you pay suppliers, the cycle is negative and suppliers are effectively funding your growth.

Should I use revenue or cost of goods sold for DIO?

Use cost of goods sold for DIO and DPO, since inventory and payables are carried at cost, and revenue for DSO.

How often should I calculate the cycle?

Quarterly is a good habit, monthly if cash is tight or you are growing fast.
