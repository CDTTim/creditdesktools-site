How it's calculated

DSCR = Annual cash flow ÷ Total annual debt service Max new loan = present value of (cash flow ÷ target − existing debt) over the new loan's term

What DSCR measures

The debt service coverage ratio compares the cash a business generates to the loan payments it owes in a year. A DSCR of 1.00x means every dollar of cash flow goes to debt, with nothing left for a slow month. A DSCR of 1.50x means the business earns $1.50 for every $1.00 of debt payments.

Lenders set a minimum, usually somewhere between 1.20x and 1.35x for conventional and SBA loans. Below it, most will decline or cut the loan amount. That is why this calculator also shows the largest new loan your cash flow supports at the lender's target. The cushion above 1.00x is there to absorb a slow quarter, a lost customer or a rise in costs without missing a payment.

Worked example

A business has $90,000 of annual cash flow available for debt. It already pays $36,000 a year on an equipment loan and a vehicle loan. It is asking for a new $250,000 loan at 9% over 10 years, and the lender's minimum is 1.25x.

  1. New loan payment: $250,000 at 9% for 120 months is about $3,166.89 a month, or $38,002.73 a year.
  2. Total debt service: $36,000 + $38,002.73 = $74,002.73.
  3. DSCR: $90,000 ÷ $74,002.73 = 1.22x.

The business covers its payments, but it falls just short of the 1.25x target. Two numbers show how far off it is:

  • Cash flow needed: 1.25 × $74,002.73 = about $92,503, roughly $2,500 more than it has.
  • Largest loan at 1.25x: $90,000 ÷ 1.25 = $72,000 of debt service allowed. Subtract the existing $36,000 and $36,000 a year, or $3,000 a month, is left for the new loan. At 9% over 10 years, that payment supports about $236,825.

So the owner could ask for about $236,000 instead of $250,000. Or, if the lender allows it, stretching the same $250,000 to 15 years lowers annual payments to about $30,428 and lifts the DSCR to about 1.35x. Run each option in the calculator above and bring the one that works to the conversation.

What counts as cash flow

Lenders start from net income and add back items that don't use cash or won't continue after the loan:

  • Depreciation and amortization
  • Interest expense on debt being refinanced or paid off
  • One-time expenses with documentation, such as a legal settlement
  • Owner compensation above a market salary, in some cases

Some lenders then subtract owner draws, taxes not already deducted, or maintenance capital spending, which is the money needed each year just to keep equipment running. Be conservative: if an add-back needs a long explanation, assume the lender won't count it.

Lenders usually work from tax returns rather than internal statements, and they may average two or three years. A strong current year after a weak one may be blended down. If this year's numbers are much better, interim financials and an explanation of what changed help.

What counts as debt service

Include every required principal and interest payment for the next twelve months: term loans, equipment loans, vehicle loans, SBA loans, and the new loan you are requesting. Lines of credit are often included at an assumed payment even if you only pay interest. Leases may be included too. Leaving debts out only means the lender finds them later.

Global DSCR

For small businesses, the owner usually guarantees the loan, so the lender also runs a global DSCR. That combines the business's cash flow with the owner's personal income, then divides by business debt plus personal obligations like a mortgage and car payments. A healthy business can still fail a global test if the owner's personal debt is heavy.

How to improve a weak DSCR

  1. Lengthen the term. A 10-year loan has a much lower annual payment than a 5-year loan of the same size.
  2. Borrow less. Use the maximum loan figure above as your ceiling.
  3. Refinance expensive debt. Rolling a short, high-payment loan into the new one can raise the ratio.
  4. Document add-backs. Clean books with clear support let the lender count cash flow they would otherwise ignore.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving out the new loan. A business with no debt shows a perfect ratio until the loan you are asking for is added.
  • Using revenue instead of cash flow. DSCR uses what is left after operating expenses, not sales.
  • Counting interest only. Debt service includes principal. A loan's full payment is what has to be covered.
  • Testing at today's rate only. If the new loan is variable, rerun the ratio a point or two higher to see how much room is left.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good DSCR?

Most lenders want at least 1.20x to 1.25x. Above 1.50x is considered strong. Below 1.00x means cash flow does not cover the payments.

Is DSCR calculated with EBITDA or net income?

Lenders typically start with net income and add back depreciation, amortization, interest and documented one-time items, which lands close to EBITDA. Each lender has its own adjustments.

Does DSCR include the new loan?

Yes. Lenders calculate DSCR on a pro forma basis, including the payment on the loan you are applying for along with your existing debt.

What is the difference between DSCR and global DSCR?

DSCR uses only the business's cash flow and debt. Global DSCR adds the guarantor's personal income and personal debt payments.
